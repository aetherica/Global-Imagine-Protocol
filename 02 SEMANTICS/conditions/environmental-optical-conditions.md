## 5W1H Orientation

### What

Environmental Optical Conditions describe the broader optical circumstances surrounding an imaging, observation, measurement, capture, or viewing context.

### Why

Optical imaging does not occur in isolation.

Atmospheric transmission, scattering, ambient radiation, background illumination, spectral environment, stray radiation, spatial distribution, and temporal variation can affect what reaches a surface, observer, or sensor.

A common environmental condition concept prevents these factors from being treated as isolated or unrelated effects.

### Who

The concept is relevant to imaging scientists, optical engineers, camera and sensor engineers, photographers, cinematographers, calibration and metrology professionals, machine-vision engineers, computational-imaging researchers, virtual-production specialists, and AI systems.

### Where

It applies to:

- outdoor and indoor scenes;
- optical laboratories;
- camera and sensor test environments;
- astronomical and remote-sensing environments;
- underwater and turbid environments;
- display/viewing environments;
- computational and simulated environments.

### When

Environmental Optical Conditions may vary with time, location, weather, source activity, motion, atmospheric state, and surrounding activity.

## Semantic Definition

**Environmental Optical Conditions** are the contextual optical circumstances surrounding a defined imaging, observation, measurement, capture, or viewing environment, including relevant radiation, transmission, scattering, background, spectral, spatial, directional, and temporal conditions.

The concept describes an environmental condition envelope rather than a single optical quantity.

## Typical Dimensions

Relevant dimensions may include:

### Ambient Radiation

Radiation present in the environment independently of the immediate primary source under investigation.

### Spectral Environment

The spectral composition or distribution of relevant radiation across the specified wavelength range.

### Spatial Distribution

The spatial variation of optical radiation across the scene, environment, or receiving region.

### Directionality

The distribution of radiation with respect to propagation or observation direction.

### Temporal Variation

Changes in optical conditions over an instant, interval, frame, sequence, or longer period.

### Atmospheric or Medium Effects

Transmission, scattering, absorption, attenuation, haze, fog, smoke, dust, water turbidity, or other medium-dependent optical effects where relevant.

### Background Illumination

Radiation contributed by surrounding regions, surfaces, or environmental sources that affects observation or measurement.

### Stray Radiation

Relevant radiation reaching a system through unintended or non-primary optical paths.

## Core Distinctions

### Environmental Optical Conditions vs Illumination Condition

Environmental Optical Conditions describe the broader environment.

Illumination Condition describes the optical influence acting on a defined receiver or region.

### Environmental Optical Conditions vs Exposure

Environmental conditions exist independently of whether a camera or sensor is currently integrating an exposure.

### Environmental Optical Conditions vs Viewing Conditions

Viewing Conditions specifically address an observer/display/viewing configuration.

An environmental optical condition may influence viewing but is not synonymous with the observer's perceptual state.

### Environmental Optical Conditions vs Weather

Weather is broader than optical conditions.

Only weather-related effects that materially influence optical propagation or observation belong to this semantic context.

## Cross-Domain Significance

Environmental Optical Conditions provide contextual information for:

- optical propagation;
- atmospheric imaging;
- remote sensing;
- camera characterization;
- sensor calibration;
- low-light imaging;
- HDR imaging;
- computational vision;
- display and viewing;
- virtual production;
- forensic analysis.

## Trust

Environmental statements should identify the relevant location, time, spectral range, direction, and receiving system whenever those dimensions materially affect interpretation.

A generic statement such as “the environment was bright” is not sufficient for technical use without contextual qualification.
